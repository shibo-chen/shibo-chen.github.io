Within Myself

October 23, 2026 · 7 min · Shibo Chen | Translations:
  • Zh

Ironman California 2026

Why Do It Again?#

In late October 2025, I went on a long run with two friends. Both were training for races. I wasn’t preparing for anything; I was just there to keep them company.

I was about six months out of my PhD and had made the transition from academia to industry. Work was settling into a rhythm, but my habits outside of it weren’t great: staying up late, eating takeout, and exercising without any consistency. I’d even signed up for a boxing gym and barely gone.

As we ran, we talked about how I might need a goal challenging enough to hold myself accountable.

A regular marathon no longer felt like a particularly high bar. I was confident I could finish one without much training. An Ironman was different. Without putting in the work, even if I finished, I knew the experience would be painful.

I was also curious. I’d finished Ironman Wisconsin in 2023. Three years later, could I still do it?

So I decided to sign up for Ironman California 2026.

In a way, I didn’t start training because I wanted to finish an Ironman. I chose an Ironman because I wanted a reason to keep training.

Facing My Fear#

When I signed up, I hadn’t realized how cold Sacramento’s river water would be in October.

Cold open water has always been my weakness. I dropped out after 500 meters in Santa Cruz. I dropped out of Swim to the Moon in Michigan, too. I barely finished Escape from Alcatraz, and when I got out of the water, I couldn’t walk in a straight line. I also threw up.

Even after finishing Ironman Wisconsin, those experiences left me with a deep fear of cold water.

This time, though, I was living in the Bay Area, with more opportunities to practice swimming in the cold. I felt I should prepare properly instead of leaving it to luck again.

I went to Santa Cruz and San Francisco’s Aquatic Park for practice swims. Both went reasonably well. Getting in was uncomfortable, but once I slowed down, I found I could gradually adjust to the cold and settle into a rhythm with my breathing and strokes.

Of course, there were new things to worry about. Wearing a trisuit under my wetsuit felt tight around my chest. Would that make breathing harder? Before the race, I spent a lot of time reading about water temperature, currents, wind direction, and breathing techniques. Looking back, I was probably overthinking some of it.

But on race day, as soon as I got in, the familiar panic came back.

The cold made my breathing rapid, and the swimmers and waves around me kept breaking my rhythm. I immediately started doubting myself. Why was this happening again? Had all that practice been for nothing? I was already considering dropping out.

Fortunately, I remembered my practice swims.

I stopped to tread water and get my breathing under control. Then I moved a little farther from the crowd to reduce the distractions and began swimming easy, using the rhythm I knew.

Slowly, my breathing settled.

I can do it.

I wasn’t fully confident yet. Only about a quarter of the way through, when I realized I wasn’t particularly tired, did I start to think:

Okay, I can at least finish this swim.

There was still plenty of uncertainty as I entered the Sacramento River. I wouldn’t say I was enjoying the swim, but at least I knew how to get through the rest of it.

That experience taught me something: preparation doesn’t guarantee a problem won’t happen again. But it can give you a way to respond when it does.

Swim Within Myself#

The Sacramento River was still crowded. Other swimmers’ strokes, kicks, and jostling for position kept disrupting my rhythm.

Eventually, I picked a less crowded line, even if it meant swimming a little farther.

My thinking was clear: my goal for the swim was to finish it safely. I’d already faced one of my fears. I didn’t need to fight everyone around me at the same time.

I’m a capable swimmer, and running is an even greater strength. There was no reason to throw off my rhythm over a few seconds in the water.

Save the battle for the land.

To me, that’s what Swim Within Myself means: knowing my goals, strengths, and limits. I don’t need to compete with everyone at every point along the course.

It doesn’t mean giving up on competition. It means choosing where to compete.

When I came out of the water, quite a few people around me were walking toward transition. I ran.

I knew I wasn’t going to place anywhere near the top. But I was there, and I wanted to give my all.

Give My All#

The bike started well. I had plenty of fuel, felt good, and was riding at roughly the speed I’d expected.

The aid stations, though, were shorter than I’d imagined and very crowded. At the first one, I tried to grab both water and a gel and missed the gel. After that, I changed my approach: focus on one type of supply at each station and take extra when I could, leaving some margin for whatever might happen later.

Heading south on the second lap, the headwind was strong, and my speed dropped considerably. I didn’t burn extra energy trying to hold my speed, but I didn’t back off my power dramatically either. I kept my output relatively steady and worked my way through the headwind.

With 20 miles left on the bike, both knees were starting to burn. I wasn’t sure how much I had left for a marathon.

When I got off, my legs felt heavy, and it was very hot. Once I actually started running, though, my pace was surprisingly decent. I kept taking gels, cooling myself down, and trying to hold it.

After 10 miles, I started to think a sub-12-hour finish might be possible. I became more deliberate about fueling, hoping I could keep going.

But at mile 16, my stomach stopped tolerating gels, and my pace gradually slowed. I realized sub-12 was probably out of reach.

I had to accept that and reset my goal to finishing. Still, I didn’t want to walk the rest of the way and draw out the pain. I kept running as much as I could manage. In the end, I ran those last few miles all the way to the finish.

Looking back, give my all doesn’t mean pushing recklessly. At different points, it can mean restraint, persistence, or accepting reality and adjusting the goal.

What matters is doing the best I can under the conditions in front of me.

Three Years Later#

When I finished Ironman Wisconsin for the first time in 2023, I was thrilled. I hadn’t known whether I could do it, so I’d raced conservatively. Only when I crossed the finish line did I finally know I’d succeeded.

This time felt different.

I had a stronger fitness base, more structured training, and a better understanding of how to pace myself. There were still plenty of difficulties along the way, but finishing the race itself was, in a way, kind of expected.

So crossing the line wasn’t as exhilarating as the first time. It was more a quiet satisfaction: the work of the past few months had paid off.

And I realized that what made me happier than finishing a second Ironman was how preparing for it had changed my life.

In the final months of training, I started going to bed and getting up early on a regular schedule, paying attention to my diet, and often training twice a day, morning and evening. At first, I made those changes to get through the race. Eventually, I found myself enjoying that healthy, consistent routine.

When I signed up, I needed a demanding goal to keep myself accountable. Now, I’d experienced the benefits and joy of a healthier life, and I knew I was capable of sticking with it.

I think I still need a goal. But it’s no longer only about keeping myself on track. I want to keep improving.

I’ve already picked the next one: a Boston qualifier.

That will be a longer challenge, requiring more accumulated training, patience, and sustained effort.

Three years ago, my first Ironman showed me I could do something difficult. This time, I came away with a better understanding of my abilities and limits, and a sense of how to find and hold a rhythm that works for me.

Within Myself.

I hope to carry that self-knowledge, along with the habits I’ve built over the past few months, into the rest of my life.

After all, an Ironman is a race that lasts a dozen or so hours. The life beyond the race is what deserves the lasting effort.
