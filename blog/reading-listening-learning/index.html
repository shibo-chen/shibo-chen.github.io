My Complicated Relationship with Reading, Listening, and Learning

November 1, 2026 · 5 min · Shibo Chen | Translations:
  • Zh

In secondary-school math classes, I would usually work through the problems I knew how to solve as quickly as possible, then spend the remaining time wrestling with the hardest ones. I didn’t necessarily follow what the teacher was explaining. I would look up and listen closely when we reached something I was stuck on.

That habit followed me to university. For many courses, I rarely paid attention in lectures, or didn’t attend at all. But if I was struggling with a subject, I would sit in class, think through it on my own, and wait for the professor to reach the parts I hadn’t figured out.

For a long time, this didn’t seem particularly remarkable to me. I had grown up in a traditional Chinese school system. Classes, exercises, and exams were what I knew. Without experiencing other environments, I rarely asked how I actually absorbed information, which methods worked for me, or which were simply familiar.

As I moved through university and into work, the environments I learned in—and the ways information reached me—kept changing. I gradually became more aware of my habits. I also started to feel that, if anything was limiting how quickly I could grow, it might be this: I am a bad, bad, bad reader and listener.

That doesn’t mean I have little interest in knowledge. Once I have a problem I want to solve, I’m hungry to understand it. I can usually work out fairly quickly what I already know, what’s missing, and where to look for answers. I become attentive, sharp, and persistent.

But that appetite depends heavily on having a question.

Without one, I struggle to remember what I’ve read or heard, or to think much further about it. I still haven’t read the Four Great Classical Novels of Chinese literature, and I read almost none of the books my English teachers recommended. At school, I often relied on films and summaries to learn what they were about.

Give me a question, though, and I’m very good at finding information, comparing explanations, and following things through until they make sense to me. That may help explain why I often did well on exams: the questions had already been supplied, and finding answers to clearly defined questions was something I was good at.

Listening to someone explain something follows a similar pattern. I like to think first and work through a problem in my own way. When I reach something I can’t figure out, I turn to someone else’s explanation. At that point, I listen very carefully. If someone interrupts, I get annoyed, sometimes angry.

At other times, if what I’m reading or hearing has little connection to the problem on my mind, I drift off easily. While my own question is still unresolved, I find it hard to give my full attention to another person or a book.

I’ve gradually come to see how problem-driven I am. This way of learning helps me get moving and make progress. Its limits have also become clearer: I often commit to an approach first, then go looking for the missing pieces.

Conic-section problems in high-school math were a good example. You could often solve them through algebraic calculation or geometric reasoning. Both approaches might work, but for a particular problem, one could be much simpler than the other. Once I had chosen a method, I wanted to keep going with it.

Even when the teacher explained a simpler solution, and I understood it, I usually still wanted to finish my own. I wanted to know whether I could reach the answer by following my line of thought.

Eventually, I found a more precise way to describe this persistence: an unfinished idea is hard for me to put down.

I can understand someone else’s approach and acknowledge that it works. But while my own remains unfinished, it keeps occupying my attention. The feeling is simple: I want to finish it.

At school, the cost might be a few more steps or some extra time. At work, where problems are more open-ended, the consequences can be greater. What is worth solving, which constraints matter most, and where to begin are all things that need exploring.

I’m still easily drawn toward finishing the path I’ve started. Even after understanding another proposal, I may spend little time investigating it further. Why did that person think of it? What did they notice that I missed? Where else could that way of thinking be useful?

I may be missing opportunities to learn.

Over time, this also shapes the knowledge I accumulate. I know what I need, so I can find it quickly. But I have little patience for information I can’t use yet, or whose connection to the current problem isn’t obvious. It might give me a different perspective in the future. Right now, I don’t know what question to use it to answer.

Writing this has made me feel that “bad reader / listener” doesn’t fully describe what’s happening. I’m good at learning with a question in mind. I’m less good at making room for other questions, especially while my own ideas are still unfinished.

I want to keep the persistence that makes me follow a thought through. Much of what I understand well, I learned by trying things along my own route and refusing to let go too soon.

I also want to learn how to pause deliberately sometimes.

Perhaps I need a clearer agenda. Before reading or entering a conversation, I could ask what I hope to understand this time. Am I trying to get past a particular obstacle, understand another approach, or explore which directions might be worth pursuing?

I’d also like to broaden the depth and variety of my questions. Alongside “How can I make my approach work?”, I could ask, “Why does someone else see it this way?” or “What questions haven’t I noticed?” And I could allow my original question to change along the way.

I haven’t turned any of this into a consistent habit yet. The unfinished idea will probably keep tugging at me. But I hope to become more deliberate about when to continue it, and when to leave it unfinished for a while and listen to the person in front of me.
